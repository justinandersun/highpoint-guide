Mauna Kea, located on the Big Island of Hawaii, is the state's highest mountain at 13,796 feet (4,205 meters), comprised of a series of volcanic cinder cones on a massive shield volcano. While Mauna Loa, the island's other prominent volcano, frequently spreads lava flows over a vast area, Mauna Kea's steeper slopes provide superior summit views and shorter hikes. It is important to recognize that Mauna Kea is a sacred site to Native Hawaiians. A sign at the base of the summit cone, Puʻu Wēkiu, asks visitors not to climb to the true summit out of respect for its sacred status. It's a request rather than a permit requirement, and we encourage highpointers to honor it.

You can travel from Hilo or the Kona/Waimea area to reach Mauna Kea. Access the Onizuka Visitor Center via Saddle Road (Highway 200). The Visitor Center (now called the Visitor Information Station, or VIS) sits at 9,200 feet, 6 miles upslope on Summit Road, near the 28-mile marker off Saddle Road. No signs mark the turn to Summit Road due to frequent theft, so careful navigation is required.

The Mauna Kea Humu'ula Trail begins approximately 100 yards northwest of the Onizuka Visitor Center. The trail is about 6.5 miles long one way (13 miles round trip) and gains about 4,700 feet (1,435 meters) from the Visitor Center to the summit. It loosely parallels a partially paved summit road. The trail follows the road from the Mauna Kea Ice Age Reserve boundary at 13,200 feet to the summit road's highpoint at 13,700 feet. The final stretch to the true summit is a short 5-to-15-minute walk with about 100 feet of gain, but please read the note on the summit cone under Regulations before continuing. Hikers will enjoy splendid and wild views along the way.

An enjoyable side trip is a short jaunt to Lake Waiau, a shallow lake about a mile from the summit. Visitors should not disturb the lake, as it holds cultural significance for Native Hawaiians.

## Regulations
No permits or fees are required to hike Mauna Kea. However, rangers request that you fill out a Visitor Information Sheet and drop it in a box next to the phone at the Visitor Center for emergencies.

A sign at the base of the summit cone, Puʻu Wēkiu, asks visitors not to climb to the true summit out of respect for its sacred status. There's no permit or permission process; it's a matter of respect. Please consider stopping at the sign.

If you drive, a dependable four-wheel-drive or all-wheel-drive vehicle is required above the Visitor Information Station. The summit road is steep, partly unpaved, and single-lane in places. For current road conditions, call (808) 935-6268.

In an emergency, call 911. Cell phone use above the VIS is limited to emergencies because of interference with the telescopes, and there's a public emergency phone at the University of Hawaiʻi 2.2-meter telescope. Maunakea Rangers patrol daily and can be reached at (808) 960-2952.

## Lodging & Camping
Camping is not allowed on Mauna Kea itself. Given the significant altitude gain from sea level, acclimatization is crucial. Consider camping at the Mauna Kea State Recreation Area, 7 miles west of Summit Road near the 35-mile marker on Saddle Road. The area is popular with hunters on weekends, so midweek visits are recommended. Alternatively, spend time at the Onizuka Visitor Center to acclimatize and learn about the various observatories on the summit.

## FAQs

### What is the best time of year to hike Mauna Kea?
The best time to hike Mauna Kea is during the dry season, from April to October. Weather conditions are more stable, and the trails are less likely to be affected by snow or heavy rain.

### Can I drive to the summit of Mauna Kea?
Yes, with a four-wheel-drive or all-wheel-drive vehicle. The partially paved road reaches the summit road's highpoint, and the last stretch to the true summit is a short walk. The summit cone is a sacred site, and a sign at its base asks visitors not to climb it; it's a request, not a permit system.

### Are there restroom facilities available?
Yes, restroom facilities are available at the Onizuka Visitor Center.

### Can I bring my dog on the hike?
No. Except for service animals (and hunting dogs under Forest Reserve rules), pets aren't allowed on Mauna Kea.

### What should I do if I experience altitude sickness?
If you experience symptoms of altitude sickness, such as headaches, dizziness, or nausea, descend to a lower elevation immediately and seek medical attention if necessary. Before the hike, acclimatizing at the Onizuka Visitor Center can help prevent altitude sickness.

### Is there a shuttle service to the summit?
There is no shuttle service to the summit. Most visitors either hike or drive to the summit's highpoint.
